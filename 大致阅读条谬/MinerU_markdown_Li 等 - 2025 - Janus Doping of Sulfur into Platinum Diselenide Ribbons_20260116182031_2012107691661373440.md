# Janus Doping of Sulfur into Platinum Diselenide Ribbons

Zechen Li, Honglin Wang, Huaipeng Wang, Yunpeng Ma, Fangzhu Qing, Xuesong Li, Qian Li, Dan Xie, and Hongwei Zhu*

2D platinum diselenide  $(\mathrm{PtSe}_2)$ , a novel member of the transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) family, possesses many excellent properties, including a layer-dependent bandgap, high carrier mobility, and broadband response, making it promise for applications in technologies like field-effect transistors and room-temperature photodetectors. Doping represents an effective method to modify the electrical properties of 2D TMDCs and to bestow upon them additional functions. However, to date, little research has been conducted on the successful doping of 2D  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  for modification. In this study, sulfur (S) powder is utilized during the chemical vapor deposition growth process of 2D  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons and successfully integrated into the  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  lattice through substitutional doping. The Au substrate significantly decreases the substitution energy of Se atoms in the lower layer of  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$ , resulting in the formation of the Janus PtSSe structure. S-doped  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons demonstrate significant symmetry breaking and enhanced electrical properties, showcasing a strong nonlinear optical response and certain synaptic plasticity, further simulating some neuromorphological processes. This study not only demonstrates a viable method for controllable doping and modification of 2D  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  but also establishes a platform for exploring the characteristics of Janus TMDCs.

Semiconducting  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  is a promising candidate for 2D electronic and optoelectronic devices, while semi metallic  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  may offer an important platform for exploring novel quantum phenomena.[2] To date, significant progress has been made in the growth of 2D  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  using methods such as chemical vapor deposition (CVD),[2,3] chemical vapor transport,[4] thermally assisted conversion,[5] molecular beam epitaxy,[6] and mechanical exfoliation.[7] However, for optimal performance, the physical properties of 2D  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  require modification. Doping serves as an effective method for adjusting material properties and device performance, encompassing strategies such as intercalation, surface charge transfer doping, and substitutional doping. Moreover, substitutional doping is recognized as the most stable and nonvolatile method.[8] By incorporating diverse elements into the lattice of 2D transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs), it is possible to effectively expand their

functions, thereby offering a novel strategy for contact engineering and carrier modulation in electronic devices based on 2D TMDCs.[9] For instance, the doping of vanadium (V) and iron atoms has endowed molybdenum disulfide  $(\mathrm{MoS}_2)$  and tungsten disulfide  $(\mathrm{WS}_2)$  with room-temperature ferromagnetism.[10]  $\mathrm{MoS}_2$  when substituted by niobium atoms, exhibited p-type transport behavior.[11] In semiconducting V-doped  $\mathrm{WSe}_2$  monolayers, room-temperature ferromagnetic domains exhibiting long-range order were identified.[12] In addition, Janus TMDC, representing a new type of asymmetric TMDC material, has recently attracted widespread research interest.[13] By replacing one layer of chalcogen X in  $\mathrm{MX}_2$  with another chalcogen Y, it is possible to form a Janus structure with the general formula MXY. Due to the disruption of planar symmetry, Janus MXY often exhibits unique electronic, structural, thermoelectric, and optical properties that differ significantly from those of 2D  $\mathrm{MX}_2$ .[14] The substitution reaction of chalcogen elements is utilized to adjust the composition of TMDC and tailor bandgaps.[15]

Over the past few years, CVD methods have achieved significant progress in the area of substitution doping of TMDCs. For instance, V atoms could enter the lattice of  $\mathrm{MoS}_2$  during CVD growth by decomposing solid V precursors, resulting in tunable doping concentrations across a wide range. The replaced V atoms were uniformly distributed in V-doped  $\mathrm{MoS}_2$ , yielding stable and uniform doping effects and electronic properties.[9]

Rhenium (Re) and V atoms could be substituted in  $\mathrm{MoS}_2$ ,  $\mathrm{WS}_2$ , and  $\mathrm{WSe}_2$  by spin coating precursor solutions onto the substrate. Tunable composition, optical, and electrical properties could be achieved in Re- and V-doped TMDC monolayers.[16] Re atoms could also be introduced into the lattice of  $\mathrm{MoS}_2$  through metalorganic CVD. The transmission characteristics of Re-doped  $\mathrm{MoS}_2$  back-gated field-effect transistors (FETs) show significant improvement compared to undoped  $\mathrm{MoS}_2$ .[8] However, due to the good stability and strong interlayer coupling,[17] current research on successfully doping 2D  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  to achieve modification is limited, although theoretical calculations suggested[18] that 2D  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  could be modified by substitutional doping.

In this study, sulfur (S) powder served as a precursor in the CVD growth of 2D  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons, facilitating the Janus doping of S atoms into 2D  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$ . According to density functional theory (DFT) calculations, Janus doping was attributed to the decrease in energy barriers during the adsorption, migration, and substitution processes of S atoms on the Au substrate. The doped ribbons demonstrated obvious symmetry breaking and significant changes in electrical properties, including the ON-OFF ratio and carrier mobility. This study presents a feasible strategy for the controllable doping and modification of 2D  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  and establishes a platform for studying the properties of Janus PtSSe and TMDCs doped with non-metallic atoms.

# 2. Results and Discussion

# 2.1. CVD Growth of S-Doped  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ( $\mathrm{PtS}_X\mathrm{Se}_{2 - X}$ ) Ribbons

Figure 1a,b depict top views of the atomic structures of  $1\mathrm{T - PtSe}_2$  and Janus 1T-PtSSe, respectively. In the top view of  $1\mathrm{T - PtSe}_2$  each Pt atom (red) is surrounded by six Se atoms (blue). The side view illustrates Pt atoms in the central layer connected to three Se atoms in both upper and lower layers (Figure S1a, Supporting Information). Complete substitution of the upper or lower Se atomic layers with S atomic layers results in Janus 1T-PtSSe (Figure S1b, Supporting Information), where each Pt atom in the top view is surrounded by three Se atoms and three S atoms (green).

An atmospheric pressure CVD method was previously developed for synthesizing 2D 1T-  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons, exhibiting high crys

To modify their physical properties and further enhance functionality, the synthesis method was refined by placing S powder as a dopant upstream of the Se powder, resulting in  $\mathrm{PtS_XSe_{2 - X}}$  ribbons (Figure 1c). The preparation process comprised two steps: 1) Employing the previously procedure, utilize  $10\mu \mathrm{L}$ $(\mathrm{NH}_4)_2\mathrm{PtCl}_4$  aqueous solution with a concentration of  $0.1 - 1\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1}$  and  $800~\mathrm{mg}$  of Se powder as precursors, reacting at  $820^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$  for  $10\mathrm{min}$  to fabricate monolayer  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons. 2) Maintaining the temperature of the heating zone constant, S powder was heated to gradually evaporate and diffuse into the reaction zone via the carrier gas, thereby the  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons were doped for  $5\mathrm{min}$ . The flow rate of the precursors was closely related to the quality of the products, and reducing the feed rate of the precursors can reduce nucleation density, thus leading to large crystal domains.[17] In this study, the temperature of the S powder was maintained at  $200^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$  to regulate the evaporation rate. It was noteworthy that the distance between the S powder and Se powder must be precisely maintained at  $5\mathrm{cm}$ . When the distance was too close, the reaction between the S powder and Pt precursors became dominant, potentially producing more by-products like  $\mathrm{PtS}_2$ . The morphologies of  $\mathrm{PtS}_2$  included irregular polygons or ribbons (Figure S2a-d, Supporting Information), enabling determination of product purity based on these morphologies. When the distance was too far, the doping effect of S atoms became poor, resulting in products that were primarily  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons.

The Au (111) substrate could play a crucial role in the Janus doping process, frequently resulting in the dissociation, chemical adsorption, and migration of gaseous S molecules. Through adsorption and migration on Au substrate, S atoms could substitute the Se layer at the bottom of  $\mathrm{MoSe}_2$  crystals, resulting in the formation of Janus MoSSe.[19] To delve deeper into the formation mechanism of the Janus PtSSe region, DFT calculations were conducted to assess the pertinent energies of the CVD reaction process (Figures S3-S5, Supporting Information). Monolayer  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbon and Au (111) supercells were constructed as the research system, which aligned with the theoretical values of the material's cell parameters. The computed adsorption energies of S atoms on the  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbon and Au (111) surface were  $-1.37$  and  $-3.79\mathrm{eV}$ , respectively, suggesting that S atom adsorption on the Au (111) surface was energetically more favorable.

The Nudged Elastic Band (NEB) curve illustrated that the migration barrier of S and Se atoms in the neighboring octahedral gaps on the Au (111) surface was low  $(\approx 0.5\mathrm{eV})$  , and they exhibited high migration rates at elevated temperatures during the CVD reaction. The substitution energy of S atoms replacing the upper and lower Se atoms in the  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbon were calculated as 3.14 and  $0.67\mathrm{eV}$  for the upper and lower Se atom substitutions, respectively, which indicated that the energy required for S atoms to replace the upper Se atoms in the  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  band was notably higher than that for replacing the lower Se atoms. Based on the calculation results, the growth mechanism of  $\mathrm{PtS}_{\mathrm{X}}\mathrm{Se}_{2 - \mathrm{X}}$  ribbons could be analyzed, which primarily comprised three processes: adsorption, migration, and substitution (Figure 1d). At high temperatures,  $\mathbf{S}_2$  molecules decomposed into S atoms, which then ad

sorbed onto the surface of Au substrates. These S atoms subsequently migrated to the vicinity of the  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons on the surface of Au substrates, replacing the lower Se atoms and forming the Janus PtSSe region.

# 2.2. Characterizations of  $\mathsf{PtS}_X\mathsf{Se}_{2\textrm{-} X}$  Ribbons

$\mathrm{PtS_XSe_{2 - X}}$  maintained ribbon morphologies, analogous to the original  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons (Figure 2a-c). The size and distribution density of  $\mathrm{PtS_XSe_{2 - X}}$  ribbons were positively correlated with the concentration of metal precursors, akin to  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons. Figure S6 (Supporting Information) illustrated a typical atomic force microscope (AFM) image of the  $\mathrm{PtS_XSe_{2 - X}}$  ribbons, revealing a

measured band thickness of  $0.92\mathrm{nm}$ , maintaining a monolayer structure. Raman spectroscopy serves as a convenient method to distinguish between doped and undoped samples, and to investigate how S atoms affect the structure and vibrational modes of  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons. Figure 2d presents the Raman spectra of pristine  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons and doped products obtained at various precursor ratios of S/Se, highlighting significant differences between doped and undoped samples. The Raman spectrum of pristine  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons featured two characteristic peaks: the  $E_{\mathrm{g}}$  peak, indicative of the in-plane vibration mode ( $181.5~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ ), and the  $A_{\mathrm{lg}}$  peak, representative of the in-plane vibration mode ( $208.5~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ ). As the precursor ratio of S/Se increased, the  $E_{\mathrm{g}}$  peak of the  $\mathrm{PtS}_{\mathrm{X}}\mathrm{Se}_{2 - \mathrm{X}}$  exhibited a slight blue shift of  $\approx 1~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$  with a larger peak width, and this shift amplified, suggesting that the introduction of S altered the in-plane structure and charge distribution. Additionally, a distinct peak ( $C_{\mathrm{S}}$ ) emerged at  $302.0~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ , sensitive to the S/Se ratios. This peak's intensity increased with a higher proportion of S, a phenomenon also observed in other doped TMDCs.[9] Interestingly, the intensity of the  $C_{\mathrm{S}}$  peak fluctuated with the rotation angle of the half-wave plate in the Raman instrument ( $\theta$ ) (Figure 2e). When  $\theta$  was set to  $0^{\circ}$  and  $180^{\circ}$ , the  $C_{\mathrm{S}}$  peak intensity reached its maximum value, whereas at  $90^{\circ}$  and  $270^{\circ}$ , it diminished, indicating that the  $C_{\mathrm{S}}$  peak intensity was sensitive to the polarization angle of the incident light, which was a distinctive characteristic distinguishing doped from undoped samples (inset of Figure 2e). In the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of  $\mathrm{PtS}_{\mathrm{X}}\mathrm{Se}_{2 - \mathrm{X}}$ , signals of substituted S atoms appeared at  $167.4~\mathrm{eV}$  ( $2p_{1/2}$ ) and  $161.6~\mathrm{eV}$  ( $2p_{3/2}$ ), suggesting an influence of S atoms on the chemical properties of  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  (Figure 2f). Furthermore, the peak area of the S signals in XPS could be utilized to calculate the content of the S element within the entire ribbon. As the S/Se ratio in the precursors was increased from 1:10 to 1:1, the proportion of S atoms rose to  $4.3\%$ ,  $10.2\%$ ,  $11.0\%$ , and  $19.7\%$ , respectively (Figure 2g). A  $10\times 10~\mu \mathrm{m}^2$  area (inset of Figure 2h) with a step size of  $0.5~{\mu\mathrm{m}}$  was selected, and Raman surface scanning conducted on the monolayer  $\mathrm{PtS}_{\mathrm{X}}\mathrm{Se}_{2 - \mathrm{X}}$  ribbon yielded a Raman spectrum containing 400 sampling points, as illustrated in Figure 2h. Using the ratio of the peak areas of  $C_{\mathrm{S}}$  and  $E_{\mathrm{g}}$  peaks as the indicator, a ratio distribution map consistent with the actual position of the ribbon was created (Figure 2i). The red area indicated a higher  $C_{\mathrm{S}}$  peak intensity, correlating to a higher doping concentration, whereas the blue indicated a higher  $C_{\mathrm{S}}$  peak intensity, correlating with a higher doping concentration, while the blue area represented a weaker  $C_{\mathrm{S}}$  peak and consequently, a lower doping concentration. The uneven intensity distribution of  $C_{\mathrm{S}}$  peaks within the ribbon suggested that S doping occurred randomly.

# 2.3. Crystal Structure and Second Harmonic Generation (SHG) of  $\mathrm{PtS}_x\mathrm{Se}_{2 - x}$  Ribbons

A high-angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscope (HAADF-STEM) was employed to study the atomic structure of monolayer  $\mathrm{PtS_XSe_{2 - X}}$  ribbons. Within the ribbons, two distinct lattice structures were observed: 1T  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  (Figure 3a), characterized by the upper and lower layers of each Pt atom bonded to three Se atoms (Figure 1a), and the Janus PtSSe structure (Figure 3b), which consisted of a Pt layer sandwiched be

tween two different S and Se layers (Figure 1b). S atoms and Se atoms could be directly observed and distinguished in HAADF-STEM images due to their dimmer contrast, stemming from the image contrast being related to the atomic number of the imaged species (Figure 3c,d). Consequently, S atoms selectively replaced regions of pristine  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons. Based on the monolayer characteristics, atomic structure, and HADDF-STEM images of  $\mathrm{PtS_XSe_{2 - X}}$  ribbons, the formation of ordered Janus PtSSe areas has been confirmed. In addition to these regions, a small number of Se vacancies and suspended Se atoms were also identified (Figure S7, Supporting Information).

Janus TMDCs can break the mirror symmetry and generate planar polarity due to charge imbalance.[20] The breaking of mirror symmetry can lead to physical effects and functionalities beyond those of traditional TMDCs, particularly in nonlinear optics, yielding powerful nonlinear responses.[21] As a nonlinear optical process, SHG can convert two photons of the fundamental frequency  $\omega$  into a single photon of the harmonic frequency  $2\omega$ ,[22,23] and is typically employed to determine lattice orientation, detect symmetry breaking, identify edge states in TMDCs[24] and analyze grains in polycrystalline films.[25] Ideal single crystal  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  is categorized within the  $D_{3\mathrm{d}}$  point group, possessing a central symmetric structure, and exhibits no SHG effect. Conversely, Janus PtSSe monolayers belong to the  $C_{3\mathrm{v}}$  point group, owing to their lack of mirror symmetry, and exhibit strong SHG signals.[26] SHG signals from monolayer  $\mathrm{PtS_XSe}_{2 - X}$  and  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  were utilized to explore the nonlinear characteristics of as-grown 2D crystals using back-reflection geometry, further enabling a comparison of the degree of symmetry breaking between doped and undoped products. The excitation source consisted of a femtosecond optical parametric oscillator laser with a repetition rate of  $76 - 80\mathrm{MHz}$ . The laser, emitting at a wavelength of  $800\mathrm{nm}$ , was nearly perpendicularly incident on the sample, with its doubling spectrum at  $400\mathrm{nm}$  being detected (Figure 4a). Polarization-dependent SHG in both monolayer  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  (Figure 4b) and  $\mathrm{PtS_XSe}_{2 - X}$  (Figure 4c) ribbons exhibited six-fold rotational symmetry, but the intensity in  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ( $\approx 6000$  cps) was significantly lower than that in  $\mathrm{PtS_XSe}_{2 - X}$  ( $\approx 150000$  cps). For crystal structures in the  $C_{3\mathrm{v}}$  point group, the SHG intensity variation is described by the equation  $I = I_0\cos 23\theta$ , where  $\theta$  is the azimuth angle between the polarization of the incident laser and the armchair direction, with  $I$  and  $I_0$  denoting the SHG intensity and its maximum response, respectively.[27] Thus, in the Janus PtSSe region with broken inversion symmetry, the SHG intensity theoretically reached its maximum value when the polarization direction aligns with one of the six armchair directions (Figure 4d), yielding a six-fold symmetric anisotropic response. As previously mentioned, perfect  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  lattices do not generate second harmonics, with the lower intensity in  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  potentially resulting from the interfacial interaction due to the Au substrate and symmetry breaking due to defects at the edges and irregularly arranged atoms.[3b] This significant enhancement of the SHG effect in  $\mathrm{PtS_XSe}_{2 - X}$  could be attributed to the introduction of non-centrosymmetric structures by Janus PtSSe regions. Furthermore, surface scan tests of monolayer  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  (Figure 4e) and  $\mathrm{PtS_XSe}_{2 - X}$  (Figure 4f) ribbons revealed that the SHG intensity distribution within a  $10\times 10\mu \mathrm{m}^2$  area mirrored the actual ribbon shape. The irregular SHG intensity distribution verified the random distribution of the doping regions, indicating that S atoms were locally concentrated within the

# 3. Conclusion

Overall, a method was developed to effectively achieve controllable doping of 2D  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons, in which S atoms were incorporated into the  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  lattice through substitutional doping, result

ing in the formation of a Janus PtSSe structure. The maximum doping concentration of S atoms reached  $19.7\%$ . Compared to undoped  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$ ,  $\mathrm{PtS_XSe_{2 - X}}$  demonstrated enhanced nonlinear optical responses, improved electrical performance, and information storage capabilities, enabling the simulation of short-term plasticity, long-term plasticity, and repetitive learning processes. Although the Janus PtSSe structure has been successfully formed in this study, preparing 2D Janus PtSSe in a large area remains challenging due to energy constraints, which implies a promising prospect for synthesizing large-scale Janus 2D TMDCs. This research offered a viable approach for investigating Janus doping modifications in 2D TMDCs and established a platform for examining the nonlinear optics of 2D TMDCs and for the design of electronic devices like artificial synaptic transistors.

# 4. Experimental Section

Materials Synthesis and Transfer.  $(\mathrm{NH}_4)_2\mathrm{PtCl}_4$  (Alfa Aesar,  $99.9\%$  purity) aqueous solution with a concentration of  $0.1 - 1\mathrm{mgmL}^{-1}$  Se powder (Alfa Aesar,  $99.5\%$  purity) with a mass of  $800~\mathrm{mg}$  and S powder (Alfa Aesar,  $99.5\%$  purity) with a mass of  $80 - 800~\mathrm{mg}$  were used as precursors, and Au foil (Alfa Aesar,  $99.99\%$  purity, thickness  $\approx 50~{\mu\mathrm{m}}$  was the growth substrate.  $\mathrm{PtSXSe}_{2\times}$  and  $\mathrm{PtSe}_2$  ribbons were synthesized in a CVD furnace (Thermo Scientific Lindberg/Blue Series).  $\mathrm{PtS_XSe_{2 - X}}$  and  $\mathrm{PtSe_2}$  ribbons grown on the Au foil were transferred to a  $\mathrm{SiO}_2 / \mathrm{Si}$  substrate by wet chemical etching. First,  $\mathrm{PtS_XSe_{2 - X} / Au}$  and  $\mathrm{PtSe_2 / Au}$  samples were coated with PMMA at  $140^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$  and then baked for  $30\mathrm{min}$  The samples were immersed in Au corrosives  $(\mathrm{KI} + \mathrm{I}_2 + \mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$  with a mass ratio of 8:2:80) and heated in a water bath at  $70^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$  for  $3\mathrm{h}$  to etch the Au. The PMMA-supported  $\mathrm{PtS_XSe_{2 - X}}$  and  $\mathrm{PtSe_2}$  were cleaned in deionized water for  $3\mathrm{h}$  and then

collected on required substrates such as  $\mathrm{SiO}_2 / \mathrm{Si}$ . Finally, the salvaged samples were heated at  $70^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$  for  $3\mathrm{h}$ , and the PMMA was dissolved in acetone at  $65^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ .

Materials Characterizations: All the samples were characterized by OM (Zeiss Axio scope A1), SEM (MERLIN VP Compact, Carl Zeiss), AFM (Cypher VRS), Raman spectroscopy (LabRAM HR Evolution, Horiba; 532 nm excitation wavelength), XPS (Escalab 250Xi, Thermo Scientific fitted with a Al K $\alpha$  as the radiation), STEM (FEI Titan 80-300; acceleration voltage:  $300\mathrm{kV}$ ) and atomic force microscope (Cypher VRS).

SHG Measurements: The excitation source was a Ti:sapphire modelocking femtosecond laser (MaiTai SP, Spectra-Physics) generating a linearly polarized laser beam at  $800\mathrm{nm}$  with a bandwidth of 30–60 nm. The  $2\omega$  signal light generated was collected in a backscattering geometry and subsequently separated using a short-pass dichroic mirror. SHG light signals were collected in a back-scattering geometry using the same objective and detected with a Hamamatsu photomultiplier tube. A zero-order half-waveplate and a Glan-Thompson polarizer were mounted on motorized rotation stages to modify the excitation light's polarization and analyze the SHG light's polarization, respectively. When their motion steps were synchronized, a virtual sample-rotation measurement scheme was implemented, eliminating off-centering issues. A pair of galvanometer mirrors scanned the incident beam's position to capture SHG microscopy images of specific square regions.

Electrical Measurements: Using Cu meshes as masks, Au was evaporated to form the source and drain electrodes on transferred  $\mathrm{PtS_xSe_{2 - x}}$  and  $\mathrm{PtSe_2}$  ribbons on  $\mathrm{SiO}_2 / \mathrm{Si}$  substrates. Subsequently, these devices were annealed at  $200^{\circ}C$  for 30 min in a tube furnace with Ar gas serving as the protective atmosphere during the annealing process. The electrical and synaptic testing of the FET devices were performed with SourceMeters (Agilent B1500A). The carrier mobility was calculated from the slope of the transfer characteristic curve using the following formula:

$$
\mu = \frac {d I _ {d s}}{d V _ {g}} \cdot \frac {L}{W C _ {g} V _ {d s}} \tag {1}
$$

where  $\mu$  represents the carrier mobility,  $\frac{dI_{ds}}{dV_g}$  is the slope from the linear portion of the transfer characteristic curve,  $L$  and  $W$  are the channel length and width in micrometers, respectively, and  $C_g$  is the gate capacitance per unit area in nF cm $^{-2}$ .  $V_{ds}$  used in the calculation was 0.1 V. The gate capacitance of the SiO $_2$ /Si substrate used in the experiments was  $\approx 3.84$  nF cm $^{-2}$ . The channel size of the FET device was determined using ImageJ software. The average channel size (L×W) for single-layer, 5-layer, and over 20-layer nanoribbons were 15.83 × 1.27, 22.32 × 2.61, and 28.05 × 3.10 μm $^2$ , respectively.

Theoretical Calculations: The study used VASP to perform DFT calculations with projector augmented wave.[41] Electron spin polarization was considered in all calculations. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional was employed for exchange-correlation effects,[42] while DFT+D3 was used for handling weak interactions.[43] The cut-off energy for the plane-wave basis was  $450\mathrm{eV}$ . K-points were sampled as  $2\times 2\times 1$  grid in the Brillouin zone by means of Monkhorst-Pack method.  $15\AA$  of layer vacuum was applied at Z-axis of slab models to avoid the Periodic effect. The transition state (TS) searches were performed using the Dimer method in the VTST package. The TS search was conducted by using the climbing-image NEB method to generate initial guess geometries, followed by the dimer method to converge to the saddle points. Energy and maximum stress were converged to  $10^{-5}\mathrm{eV}$  and  $0.02\mathrm{eV}\mathring{\mathrm{A}}^{-1}$ , respectively.
